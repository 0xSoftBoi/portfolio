Correctness under hidden failure modes

The systems I find most interesting are the ones that pass the obvious test and fail the important one.

StatusWorking thesis
EvidenceObserved + derived + targets
MethodCross-project case synthesis
StandardResearch loop v1.0 ↗

Claim. In several apparently unrelated engineering failures, the common mistake is not simply “insufficient testing.” It is testing an abstraction as though it were the underlying system. The practical remedy is to identify the cheapest experiment capable of making the abstraction disagree with reality, then label the strength of the resulting evidence.

A unit test can prove that a model of a NIC behaves correctly. It cannot prove that the NIC does. A benchmark can produce a precise score while accidentally measuring information leaked by its own prompt. An uncertainty estimator can return a number for every prediction without that number containing useful information about error. A distributed protocol can satisfy its happy-path specification while failing under an adversarial transition nobody modeled.

I used to think these were different classes of engineering problem. Increasingly, I think they are the same problem: an abstraction has quietly become the object being tested.

The job is not to make the abstraction pass. The job is to find the cheapest experiment capable of proving the abstraction wrong.

Hardware is an adversarial reviewer

While building RDMA/RoCE diagnostic tooling, 197 unit tests could establish internal consistency across a large portion of the software. Real RDMA execution then exposed four defects that the unit-test environment did not. That does not imply unit tests were useless. It implies that I had allowed evidence from the model to stand in for evidence from the device.

The design rule that followed is simple: every reported fact should say whether it was observed, derived, estimated, or simulated. If a check requires hardware, an analysis-only substitute should not be presented as equivalent evidence.

Method note
This case is a software/hardware validation postmortem, not a randomized comparison of testing strategies. “Four defects” establishes that the hardware path added information; it does not estimate the general defect-detection rate of hardware CI versus unit testing.

A benchmark can be wrong even when the code is right

In BRIDGE-bench, 13 of 24 prompts contained information about the intended bug. That creates a direct threat to construct validity: a model can appear better at detecting a vulnerability partly because the evaluation tells it what to look for. Once that was identified, the defensible response was not to preserve the original headline score with a footnote. It was to demote the result and define a paired raw → stripped → anonymized rerun.

Evaluation work is dangerous because numbers acquire authority faster than experimental designs do. A benchmark should therefore be treated like production infrastructure: threat-model the information path, test its invariants, and assume that convenient context can leak into the metric.

Next measurement
The current claim is about contamination, which is already observable in the prompts. The stronger causal claim—how many points of performance were caused by that contamination—remains open until the sanitized paired rerun is complete.

Uncertainty is not useful because it has units

In the materials discovery experiments, a pretrained M3GNet surrogate produced a strong ranking signal: discovery acceleration factor was roughly 5.15× for greedy mean ranking and 5.17× for UCB, versus about 1.01× for random screening. The apparently sophisticated conclusion would be that uncertainty-aware acquisition was helping.

Calibration contradicted that story. The MC-dropout uncertainty estimate had Spearman correlation −0.47 with absolute prediction error. UCB was barely different from greedy because the useful signal was the mean prediction, while the uncertainty term added little useful acquisition information. The negative result narrowed the mechanism without erasing the useful performance result.

Interpretation boundary
This result is specific to the tested surrogate, dropout construction, dataset, and acquisition setup. It does not establish that MC dropout is universally useless or that all uncertainty-aware materials acquisition fails.

Delete ambition until a claim becomes testable

Aiur started as a deliberately excessive concept: persistent lighter-than-air infrastructure acting as a carrier for autonomous drones. But the impressive architecture is not yet evidence. The current P0 program removes the giant vehicle, airborne data center, charging system, and swarm behavior until one physical question remains: can a buoyant carrier repeatedly recover a moving micro-UAV onto a mechanically positive dock?

The thresholds—at least 50 instrumented attempts, at least 90% capture success inside the declared envelope, and zero unsafe contacts—are targets, not results. That distinction matters. A future failure to meet them should kill or redesign the recovery architecture rather than be written around.

The strongest counterargument

A simpler explanation for these cases is that they are merely examples of ordinary test coverage problems: add more integration tests, improve benchmark hygiene, calibrate the model, prototype the hardware. That explanation is partly correct. The reason I think the abstraction framing adds something is that it predicts where to look next: identify which proxy has silently inherited the authority of the underlying system, then design a boundary-crossing test specifically against it.

That framing is useful only if it changes engineering behavior. If it merely renames “test more realistically,” it has no independent value. The next articles in this series should therefore test the framework case by case rather than assuming this synthesis is general.

What would change my mind?

Falsification / update conditions
If the sanitized BRIDGE-bench rerun preserves the original result almost unchanged, contamination becomes less central to that case. If repeated RoCE hardware qualification finds no classes of failure beyond those captured in deterministic simulation, the hardware boundary is less informative than claimed. If calibrated uncertainty or an alternative uncertainty method materially outperforms mean-only acquisition in the materials setup, the present mechanism should be revised. If Aiur cannot meet its declared P0 gate, the carrier-recovery claim fails rather than graduating into a success narrative.

The common rule

Across networks, ML evaluation, scientific computing, cryptographic systems, and autonomous hardware, I now use roughly the same evidence hierarchy: direct reproducible observation is stronger than a derived result; a derived result is stronger than a model-dependent estimate; and a target is not evidence at all until measured.

This makes engineering less comfortable. It can also make it faster. A good negative result can kill an entire branch of work. A real-hardware failure can invalidate weeks of simulated confidence. A contaminated benchmark can stop optimization against a fiction.

I want tests that can embarrass the implementation, benchmarks that can embarrass the hypothesis, and hardware that can embarrass both.

That is the thread connecting the projects I want to keep working on: systems where correctness is not obvious, where the abstraction is useful but untrustworthy, and where measurement can still change the conclusion.
